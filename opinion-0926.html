Impact of aviation on global climate change

Catalyzing the new field of Earth System science, Johan Rockström presented in the year 2009 clear evidence underscoring the urgency of preventing human activities from causing unacceptable environmental and climatic change. Early warning signs were provided by the Club of Rome in 1972 in their landmark study The Limits to Growth and by individuals such as Hoimar von Ditfurth in the popular German TV science show Querschnitt in 1978 and Carl Sagan in his U.S. congressional testimony in 1985.

That global aviation significantly contributes to climate change was scientifically established and politically recognized over the course of the 1990s. Research prior to and during this period focused on the atmospheric impacts of aircraft nitrogen dioxide emissions on atmospheric ozone. Scientific studies on contrail-induced cloudiness and its climate impact were only just emerging. Intense international research efforts culminated 1999 in the widely accepted Special Report by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Since then, the scientific community has intensively investigated climate effects of both contrail cloudiness and the contribution of global aviation to anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions.

Reducing aviation CO2 emissions and contrails reduces the global warming contribution due to air traffic. It is scientifically well established that CO2 molecules warm the atmosphere over centuries. Because atmospheric effects of clouds are transient, reducing them appears to be suitable for short-term mitigation. However, there is a catch: predictions of the degree of warming due to contrail cloudiness carry large uncertainties.

Two options to reduce the contrail impact have come under closer scrutiny: aviation fuel composition changes and operational contrail avoidance. While some contend that the implementing the latter option immediately is critical for safeguarding the future climate, others suggest that reducing contrails by tactically rerouting aircraft to avoid contrail formation could counterproductively hinder climate change mitigation. A commentary published by the Global Catastrophic Risk Institute highlights the current lack of consensus among scientists on whether actively avoiding long-lived contrails yields a net climate benefit.

To prevent increasing human suffering and loss of biodiversity beyond 2100 requires reaching and sustaining net zero anthropogenic CO2 emissions as soon as possible in order to meet the 2°C guard-rail temperature target. Much of this increase in global mean surface temperature has already occurred: the first half of 2026 averaged roughly 1.4°C above pre-industrial levels. This means that every tenth of a degree of less warming matters.

On average, more than 100,000 flights depart from airports daily. In the year 2018, aviation's contribution to human-induced global warming was 4% and it may well increase further over the next decades. However, with a moderate reduction of air traffic emissions of 2.5% per year alone—less than required by most other economic sectors to meet their climate targets—aviation's contribution to global warming is projected to reach a constant level in 2050. Additionally implementing contrail mitigation strategies may yield further reductions in this level, provided they do not elevate CO2 emissions. Meeting aviation climate targets requires major technological and lifestyle shifts. Managing this transition effectively relies on a sound understanding of atmospheric processes and robust climate predictions.
