Forstenried Laboratory for Cloud Physics
(FoCP)

First documented in 1166 as a woodland clearing, the southern Munich district of Forstenried grew around the late-Gothic Heiligkreuz church, anchoring the settlement within Bavaria's monastic network. In this region, monastic estates and forestries collaborated on agricultural and weather tracking. Monastic scholars were instrumental in early meteorology. Forstenried is tangentially related to monastic weather research through the assignment of the Polling Abbey canon and astronomer Prosper Goldhofer to the local parish in 1765.

Mission

The FoCP has been researching clouds since its inception in 2026, complementing ongoing research of Bernd Kärcher (CV, Bio) at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) by providing non-financial support through the organisation of scientific cooperation and the provision of technical infrastructure.

Opinion

Sep 2026
Catalyzing the new field of Earth System science, Johan Rockström presented in his highly influential publication compelling evidence as to the urgency to prevent human activities from causing unacceptable environmental and climatic change. Warning signs were provided by the Club of Rome as early as 1972 in their landmark study The Limits to Growth, followed later by individuals such as Hoimar von Ditfurth in the popular German TV science show Querschnitt in 1978 and Carl Sagan in his U.S. congressional testimony in 1985.
That global aviation significantly contributes to climate change was scientifically established and politically recognized over the course of the 1990s. Intense international scientific research efforts culiminated in the widely accepted 1999 special report by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) titled 'Aviation and the Global Atmosphere'.
While some scientists contend that navigational contrail avoidance is critical for safeguarding the future climate, others suggest that reducing them by re-routing contrail-generating aircraft could counterproductively hinder climate change mitigation. A commentary published by the Global Catastrophic Risk Institute (GCRI) highlights the current lack of consensus among scientists on whether actively avoiding contrails yields a net climate benefit.
In my own opinion: To prevent increasing human suffering and loss of biodiversity beyond 2100 requires reaching and sustaining net zero anthropogenic CO2 emissions, including those from global air traffic, as soon as possible in order to meet the 1.5oC guard-rail temperature target. Much of this increase in global mean surface temperature has already occurred, so every tenth of a degree of less warming matters. Aircraft-induced cloudiness is currently believed to dominate today's overall aviation-induced warming, but uncertainties in predictions connected with contrail processes are large. While CO2 emissions from aviation remain active over centuries and millennia, the warming effect of contrail cirrus clouds is transient.

Reducing CO2 emissions ideally in concert with mitigation of the contrail climate impact directly reduces the global warming contribution due to aviation. On average, more than 100,000 flights depart from airports daily. In the year 2018, aviation's contribution to human-induced global warming was 4% and it may well increase further over the next decades. However, with a moderate reduction of air traffic emissions of 2.5% per year—less than required by other economic sectors to meet their climate targets—aviation's contribution to global warming is projected to reach a constant level in 2050.
Meeting aviation climate targets requires major technological and lifestyle shifts. Managing this transition effectively relies on a precise understanding of atmospheric processes and robust climate model predictions.
